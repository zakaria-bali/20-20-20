Inside your eye

What staring at a screen asks of your eye

To see something close, a ring of muscle around the lens (the ciliary muscle) squeezes, and the lens bulges to bend light more. Looking far away lets that muscle let go and the lens flatten. Move the slider or play a break to see it.

Diopters measure how hard the eye focuses: 1 divided by the distance in metres.

Ciliary muscle Squeezing to hold focus

Held like this for hours, the muscle gets tired. That is the heavy, achy feeling at the end of a screen day.

Why it helps

Twenty seconds that undo three small habits

A 20-second break is rest, not a workout. Looking far away gives your focusing muscle a moment off, so it starts the next 20 minutes fresh.

1.67 D → 0.17 D

Your focusing muscle relaxes

At screen distance the eye works at about 1.7 diopters of effort. At 6 m it drops to almost nothing. That release is what eases the tired, strained feeling.

Blink, blink

You start blinking again

People blink less while looking at screens, and blinking is what keeps the surface of the eye moist. A pause from the screen brings natural blinking back and helps with dry, gritty eyes.

Eyes uncross

Your eyes stop turning inward

To look at something close, both eyes rotate slightly toward your nose and hold there. Looking far lets them point straight again, so those muscles get a break too.

The research

The habit only works while something reminds you

In a 2022 study published in Contact Lens & Anterior Eye, 29 computer users with eye strain used software that reminded them to follow the 20-20-20 rule for two weeks. They took more frequent breaks, and their eye strain and dry eye symptoms went down. One week after the reminders stopped, the improvement was gone.

That is the reason this extension exists: it keeps reminding you, so the benefit keeps going. Read the study.
